# Othello – annotated model essay and self-marking guide

::: warning Study aid, not a script
This is an original model written for revision, not a QCAA sample. The exam question is **unseen**: learn the *moves* (thesis, evidence, technique, effect, context, synthesis), not the wording. Check quotes against your edition. The exam asks for **800–1000 words** in 120 minutes (plus 15 minutes' planning). This model is about 800–850 words, so use it as a length and depth guide.
:::

**Prompt:** *"Othello is destroyed by his own flaws, not by Iago." Discuss.*

---

## The essay

### Introduction
Shakespeare's *Othello* presents a tragedy in which villainy and vulnerability cannot be separated. Although Iago's calculated manipulation drives the plot, Shakespeare ultimately shows Othello's downfall to be the product of his own credulity and insecurity, flaws that Iago diagnoses and exploits but does not create. Through soliloquy, dramatic irony and the disintegration of Othello's language, the play invites its audience to hold both men responsible, while questioning the Venetian society that makes its outsider doubt his worth.

> **Why it works:** it answers the prompt directly, takes a clear viewpoint ("largely his own flaws, but shared"), names the techniques to be analysed, and signals the *perspective* (society) that lifts the response above a simple "flaw vs villain" binary.

### Body paragraph 1 – Iago is the architect, but he only finds the weakness
Iago's soliloquies establish him as the engineer of destruction, yet they also reveal that he is merely reading Othello's character. His observation that "The Moor is of a free and open nature, / That thinks men honest that but seem to be so" (1.3) is delivered directly to the audience, positioning us as complicit confidants who watch the plan form. The animal imagery of "led by th' nose as asses are" exposes Iago's contempt, but the diagnosis is accurate: Othello's trusting nature already exists before Iago arrives. Shakespeare's use of **soliloquy** therefore does double work, condemning Iago's malice while locating the *raw material* of the tragedy within Othello. Iago's declaration "I am not what I am" (1.1), an inversion of God's "I am that I am", confirms him as the deceiver, but deception succeeds only where there is a willingness to believe.

> **Why it works:** a topic sentence that uses the prompt's key words, two short embedded quotes, named techniques (soliloquy, imagery, inversion), an effect on the audience (complicity), and a link back to the thesis ("raw material").

### Body paragraph 2 – Othello's demand for certainty collapses into credulity
Othello's own flaw becomes visible in the contradiction between what he demands and what he accepts. He insists on "ocular proof" (3.3) and threatens Iago with "Villain, be sure thou prove my love a whore", yet he is satisfied by a handkerchief and a staged overhearing of Cassio in Act 4, rejecting Desdemona's testimony. Shakespeare marks this decline through **form**: the controlled blank verse of Othello's Act 1 speech to the Senate gives way to the fragmented prose of "Handkerchief—confessions—handkerchief!" (4.1). The repetition and broken syntax mirror a mind losing its grip, so that the audience witnesses not Iago's hand but Othello's own judgement failing. The **dramatic irony** of "Chaos is come again" (3.3), spoken in love, reveals that his identity rests on certainty in Desdemona; when that certainty is shaken he chooses violence over inquiry, a choice no villain can make for him. His demand for "ocular proof" also exposes a double standard: a handkerchief and Iago's report are enough to condemn Desdemona, while her repeated denials in 4.2 and 5.2 count for nothing, so the burden of proof falls on the one character who has done nothing wrong.

> **Why it works:** it moves from event to technique (verse to prose) to effect (the audience watches judgement fail), and uses a second technique (dramatic irony) to extend the argument. The final clause makes the evaluative point about *choice*.

### Body paragraph 3 – Society shapes the flaw, but does not excuse it
Shakespeare complicates the question by suggesting that Othello's insecurity is partly learned. His admission "Haply, for I am black" (3.3), with its hesitant opening, shows him internalising the prejudice voiced by Brabantio ("Look to her, Moor, if thou hast eyes to see: / She has deceived her father, and may thee") and by Iago's dehumanising language ("an old black ram", 1.1). The **animal imagery** that Venetian men apply to Othello becomes the lens through which he comes to see himself. In a Jacobean context in which outsiders were viewed with suspicion and a woman's honour was a man's possession, Iago's insinuations land on prepared ground. Yet Shakespeare refuses to let Othello hide behind this context. In the final act, "It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul" presents the murder as ritual justice, and "one that loved not wisely but too well" attempts to author a flattering legacy. The audience, invited to pity him, is also positioned to recognise the self-deception.

> **Why it works:** it introduces a *cultural assumption/value* (Objective 4), links context to quotes, and evaluates Othello's final speech critically, which shows an "informed and critical perspective".

### Body paragraph 4 – Other characters show that Othello's response was a choice
Shakespeare strengthens the case against Othello's own judgement by placing him beside characters who meet the same deception and respond differently. Desdemona, who loved Othello because she "saw Othello's visage in his mind" (1.3), judges him by his character rather than his colour and never turns on him, even as he strikes her. Emilia, who hands the handkerchief to Iago without knowing his purpose, is the one voice who insists "Let husbands know / Their wives have sense like them" (4.3) and, in the final scene, exposes Iago aloud. Both women are as exposed to Iago's lies as Othello is, yet their loyalty and eventual defiance show that another response was possible, so that his rage reads as a decision rather than an inevitability. At the same time, their fates, Desdemona murdered and Emilia killed by Iago, remind the audience that a patriarchal order lets men act on suspicion while women pay for being believed too late.

> **Why it works:** it uses *foils* to support the thesis (Objective 3), adds a gender perspective, and ends by linking back to values. It also gives the essay a fourth body paragraph, which brings the model close to the 800–1000 word range. Add detail to reach about 900.

### Conclusion
Ultimately, *Othello* resists the comfort of a single cause, and refuses the audience the relief of blaming one man. Iago supplies the poison, but Shakespeare shows Othello's credulity, insecurity and need for certainty to be what make him drink it, while a prejudiced society prepares the cup. By aligning the audience first with Iago's plot and then with Othello's collapse, Shakespeare demands that we judge both the manipulator and the man who chooses to be manipulated, and consider how easily trust is turned into destruction.

> **Why it works:** it *synthesises* rather than summarises, restates the viewpoint in new words, and ends on what the play invites the audience to consider.

## Self-marking guide
::: tip How to use
After a timed essay, tick what you did. This is a revision tool; QCAA's official marking guide is separate. Ask your teacher to mark against the official criteria.
:::

| Aspect | Strong (aim for this) | Needs work |
|---|---|---|
| **Thesis** | Answers the prompt, clear viewpoint, signalled in the intro | Vague, retells the plot, ignores the question |
| **Evidence** | 2–3 short embedded quotes per paragraph; accurate | Long quotes, no quotes, or quotes dropped in without integration |
| **Analysis of technique** | Names the device *and* explains the effect on the audience | Lists devices only ("Shakespeare uses imagery") |
| **Context/values** | Links to race, gender, honour, power in Jacobean/Venetian society | None, or a context fact unrelated to the argument |
| **Structure** | Each paragraph = point → evidence → technique → effect → link to thesis | Paragraphs that summarise events |
| **Cohesion** | Key words from the prompt recur; connectives show logic | Paragraphs unconnected; repeated "also" and "another" |
| **Language** | Formal, precise, varied sentences, accurate spelling and punctuation | Informal phrases, repetition, errors that distract |
| **Perspective** | Considers more than one reading (e.g. flaw, Iago, society) | One-dimensional argument |

## Upgrade checklist
- [ ] Every topic sentence uses a key word from the prompt
- [ ] At least **eight** short quotes in total, each embedded in a sentence
- [ ] At least **five** different named techniques
- [ ] At least one sentence of **evaluation** ("Shakespeare refuses to…", "the audience is invited to…")
- [ ] A **counter-argument** acknowledged and answered
- [ ] The conclusion **synthesises**, not summarises
- [ ] 5 minutes left to proofread: spelling of names, apostrophes, subject-verb agreement

## Practise from this model
1. **Cover and rewrite:** read one body paragraph, hide it, then write your own version using different quotes.
2. **Swap the prompt:** keep the structure but re-answer *"Othello is a play about the destructive power of manipulation."* using the [essay plan](/english/essay-plans).
3. **Timed:** plan for 15 minutes, then write for 120 (800–1000 words). Self-mark with the table above.
