## How to use each prompt
1. **Plan in 15 minutes**: thesis, 3 paragraph ideas, 2–3 short quotes each, one context point.
2. **Write the essay** in 800–1000 words (about 120 minutes including checking).
3. **Mark with the guide**: *Knowledge application* (perspectives, ideas that underpin the text, writer's choices), *Organisation and development* (thesis, evidence, sequencing, cohesion), *Textual features* (grammar, language, punctuation, spelling).

## 2024 (a): "How is the reader invited to view the concept of jealousy in the play?"

<Diagram name="sol-eng-2024a" />

**Thesis:** *Othello* invites the reader to see jealousy as a self-generating, destructive force that exploits insecurity, one that is born in Iago's resentment, nourished by Othello's need for certainty, and fatal to all it touches.

| Paragraph | Idea | Evidence and technique | Context / values |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Jealousy as a manipulated "monster"** | Iago's warning "O, beware, my lord, of jealousy! / It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on" (3.3): personification and dramatic irony, since he plants what he names | Jacobean fear of cuckoldry and honour tied to a wife's chastity |
| 2 | **Jealousy needs no cause** | Emilia: "jealous souls will not be answered so; / They are not ever jealous for the cause, / But jealous for they are jealous; 'tis a monster / Begot upon itself" (3.4): the self-feeding image; and "Trifles light as air / Are to the jealous confirmations strong" (3.3) | Reader invited to see irrationality, not justice |
| 3 | **Jealousy destroys the jealous and the innocent** | Othello's collapse in fragmented prose (4.1); Desdemona's death; Iago's own motive: "it is thought abroad that 'twixt my sheets / He's done my office" (1.3) | Evaluation: Shakespeare invites pity and judgment of both men |

**Model paragraph (paragraph 2):**
> Shakespeare invites the reader to understand jealousy not as a response to evidence but as a condition that creates its own proof. Emilia's diagnosis, that "jealous souls … are not ever jealous for the cause, / But jealous for they are jealous", is delivered in a scene where Desdemona is genuinely innocent, so that the audience, who know the truth, watch Othello's suspicion feed on nothing. The image of a "monster / Begot upon itself" turns jealousy into a self-reproducing creature, echoing Iago's earlier "green-eyed monster", and its cyclical syntax mirrors the way suspicion circles in Othello's mind. Iago's own claim that "trifles light as air" are "to the jealous confirmations strong" completes the pattern: the less evidence there is, the more it is believed. In a society where a man's honour depended on his wife's fidelity, such logic is dangerous, and Shakespeare positions the reader to recognise how readily social anxiety turns private doubt into violence.

**Upgrade angle:** contrast **Iago's envy** (professional and sexual resentment) with **Othello's jealousy** (insecurity as an outsider), and note Cassio and Bianca's mild jealousy as a counterpoint.

## 2024 (b): "Analyse the significance of Desdemona's handkerchief in the play."

<Diagram name="sol-eng-2024b" />

**Thesis:** The handkerchief is *Othello*'s central symbol: a love token that Iago converts into "ocular proof", a measure of how objects replace people as evidence, and a marker of the way women are treated as property.

| Paragraph | Idea | Evidence and technique | Context / values |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Symbol of love and bond** | Othello's account: "That handkerchief / Did an Egyptian to my mother give" (3.4), with its magic and "mummy" of "maidens' hearts": the exotic, sacred origins | Gender: Desdemona holds the "love" while Othello treats the gift as a ritual guarantee of fidelity |
| 2 | **Iago's tool: trifle becomes proof** | Emilia gives it to Iago; Iago will "in Cassio's lodging lose this napkin" (3.3); "Trifles light as air"; Othello's demand for "ocular proof" | Irony: the more a sign is empty, the more it controls |
| 3 | **Collapse of Othello's language** | "Handkerchief—confessions—handkerchief!" (4.1): fragmentation, repetition, dropping into prose; Bianca flings it at Cassio | The handkerchief becomes a stand-in for Desdemona's chastity and body: objectification |

**Model paragraph (paragraph 2):**
> Shakespeare makes the handkerchief significant precisely because it is trivial. Iago's plan to "lose this napkin" in Cassio's lodging and his remark that "trifles light as air / Are to the jealous confirmations strong" show him understanding that Othello's mind, once suspicious, will read any object as evidence. The handkerchief therefore carries the play's dramatic irony: the audience watch it pass from Desdemona's hand to Emilia's, to Iago's, to Cassio's, and finally to Bianca's, a chain of mishandling that no character except Iago fully controls. Othello's demand for "ocular proof" ironically accepts a piece of cloth in place of the woman who loves him. In making a symbol decisive, Shakespeare critiques a society in which a woman's honour is judged by objects and a man's certainty is built on what he is told.

**Upgrade angle:** link to **structure** (handkerchief lost in 3.3, mentioned in 3.4, used in 4.1, quoted in 5.2) and to the **role of Emilia**.

## 2025 (a): "What comment does the play make about the importance of reputation?"

<Diagram name="sol-eng-2025a" />

**Thesis:** *Othello* comments that reputation is a fragile social currency, easily stolen or counterfeited, and that those who value appearances over truth, from Cassio to Othello himself, are most vulnerable to destruction.

| Paragraph | Idea | Evidence and technique | Context / values |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Reputation as identity** | Cassio: "Reputation, reputation, reputation! O, I have lost my reputation! I have lost the immortal part of myself" (2.3): repetition, hyperbole | Venetian military honour culture |
| 2 | **Iago manipulates reputation** | His "good name" speech: "Who steals my purse steals trash … But he that filches from me my good name / Robs me of that which not enriches him / And makes me poor indeed" (3.3), delivered while he destroys others' names. Irony of "honest Iago" | Appearance vs reality |
| 3 | **Othello and Desdemona's names** | "Her name, that was as fresh / As Dian's visage, is now begrimed and black / As mine own face" (3.3): race and reputation fused. Final speech: "Speak of me as I am… one that loved not wisely but too well" (5.2) | Othello as outsider needs approval; he tries to write his own legacy |

**Model paragraph (paragraph 3):**
> For Othello, reputation is not only social but racial. His image of Desdemona's name as once "fresh / As Dian's visage" and now "begrimed and black / As mine own face" fuses her honour with his own colour, revealing how deeply he has absorbed Venice's prejudices. As an outsider, whose position depends on his reputation as a general, he cannot tolerate the idea that his name might be diminished by a faithless wife. Shakespeare then reverses this concern in the final scene, where Othello asks to be spoken of "as I am", trying to author his own reputation even as he confesses to murder. The audience is invited to pity a man so dependent on others' views, yet also to question whether such an appeal to posterity is another form of vanity.

**Upgrade angle:** Iago's irony — he has "honest" as his reputation — shows reputation as **performance**, and Cassio's recovery of lieutenancy shows it can be repaired.

## 2025 (b): "Analyse the significance of Emilia's character in the play."

<Diagram name="sol-eng-2025b" />

**Thesis:** Emilia is significant as a foil to Desdemona, as the unwitting agent of the handkerchief plot, and as the voice who exposes the play's patriarchal double standards and finally speaks the truth that destroys Iago.

| Paragraph | Idea | Evidence and technique | Context / values |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Foil and realist** | Desdemona's idealism against Emilia's pragmatism: "They are all but stomachs, and we all but food; / They eat us hungerly, and when they are full / They belch us" (3.4): metaphor of consumption | Gender: marriage as appetite and power |
| 2 | **The handkerchief and complicity** | She takes the handkerchief "to please his fantasy" (3.3) for Iago. Dramatic irony: she is both victim and tool of his control | Wifely obedience and its cost |
| 3 | **Voice of truth** | "Let husbands know / Their wives have sense like them" (4.3); in 5.2 "O, the more angel she, / And you the blacker devil!", "I will not charm my tongue; I am bound to speak", and "Moor, she was chaste" | She defies her husband and dies for the truth, giving the play its moral turning point |

**Model paragraph (paragraph 3):**
> Emilia's significance culminates in the final scene, where she transforms from compliant wife to the play's moral witness. Having earlier protested that "Let husbands know / Their wives have sense like them", she now refuses Iago's command to be silent, declaring "I will not charm my tongue; I am bound to speak". The shift from the short, obedient replies of the earlier acts to extended, defiant speech marks her emancipation, and the antithesis "the more angel she, / And you the blacker devil!" completes the play's inversion of black and white imagery, assigning evil to the supposedly honest Iago. Shakespeare grants her the truth-telling that no male character can deliver, and in a society that expects women's silence, her death for speaking suggests both the cost of resistance and the play's critique of patriarchal authority.

**Upgrade angle:** discuss how her late action *comes too late* and ties to Othello's decision to "believe" rather than inquire.

## Practise the wider pattern (2020–2025)
Othello has only four real past prompts, so also practise the question types the other texts have faced: **"how does the ending invite the audience to view [protagonist]?"** (five texts in 2022), a **character's significance**, a **relationship**, a **symbol**, a **"To what extent do you agree?"** statement, and **"What point of view about [trust/loyalty/family] is communicated?"**. See the table of tragedy prompts adapted to Othello in [past papers analysis](/english/past-papers#_2020-2022-and-2023-papers-other-texts-as-othello-practice).

## Checklist for any past prompt
- [ ] Thesis uses the prompt's key word (jealousy / handkerchief / reputation / Emilia)
- [ ] Each paragraph: point, short embedded quote, named technique, effect on the reader, link to context or values
- [ ] At least one paragraph on **cultural assumptions** (gender, race, honour)
- [ ] A **counter-view or complication** acknowledged
- [ ] **Synthesising conclusion**, not a summary
- [ ] 800–1000 words, formal register, accurate punctuation
